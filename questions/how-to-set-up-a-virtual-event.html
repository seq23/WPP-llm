How to Set Up a Virtual Event Without Losing Your Mind

Setting up a virtual event is the complete process of planning, testing, and broadcasting a live online gathering like a webinar, summit, or town hall. It is the method of combining software, hardware, and human coordination to deliver reliable video and audio to remote audiences.

Published 6 November 2026 · West Peek Productions

Start with the format and audience

Before you touch any software, define what you are actually building. Is this a short, slide-heavy webinar for customers, an internal town hall with hundreds of employees, or a multi-day hybrid summit with live and pre-recorded tracks? The format dictates your technical requirements, your speaker preparation, and your run-of-show. Write down your core goal and stick to it.

Next, map out the attendee journey. How do they register? Where do they land on the day of the event? What happens if they lose their internet connection? Thinking through these details early prevents last-minute panic. You can explore our foundational breakdown on What Is Virtual Event Production for a closer look at how these pieces fit together.

Keep your agenda realistic. Virtual fatigue sets in much faster than in-person fatigue. Break long days into digestible segments, leave plenty of buffer time between sessions, and always include scheduled breaks for your audience and your production crew.

Choose the right technology stack

The software you use depends on the level of polish you need. Simple video conferencing tools work for internal meetings, but public-facing broadcasts often require dedicated event platforms with custom branding, moderation tools, and reliable streaming architecture. Test your chosen platform thoroughly on the exact computers and networks your team will use.

Hardware matters just as much as software. Do not rely on built-in laptop microphones and webcams for a professional production. Invest in dedicated USB microphones, clear lighting, and hardwired ethernet connections for every presenter. If you are bringing in speakers from remote locations, send them a simple gear kit ahead of time and run a technical check at least 48 hours before going live.

  • Use hardwired internet connections instead of Wi-Fi.
  • Test audio levels and screen sharing with every speaker.
  • Ensure your registration page matches your brand guidelines.
  • Prepare backup slides and pre-recorded videos for emergencies.

Build a detailed run-of-show

A run-of-show is the minute-by-minute script of your event. It lists every action, cue, slide change, speaker transition, and backup plan from the moment the stream goes live to the final sign-off. Share this document with all producers, speakers, and moderators so everyone knows their exact cue.

Run at least one full technical rehearsal with all speakers present. Practice bringing slides up, switching between speakers, and handling simulated technical failures. When everyone has walked through the agenda at least once, the live day runs with significantly less stress and fewer mistakes.

For guidance on how to get people to actually show up once your production is locked in, read our guide on Virtual Event Marketing: Promote Without Breaking Production. It covers practical ways to build anticipation without draining your team's time.

Manage the live broadcast

On the day of the event, your primary job is oversight. Assign specific roles so no single person is trying to admit attendees, manage slides, monitor the chat, and direct speakers all at once. Have a dedicated producer who does nothing except watch the stream feed and communicate with the speakers behind the scenes.

Keep a dedicated virtual green room where speakers can gather before going live. This keeps them calm, allows you to check their audio one last time, and prevents background noise from leaking into the main broadcast. For a deeper look at how to structure these technical workflows across different event types, visit Host Virtual Events | VirtualAgency OS.

When the event ends, do not cut the stream instantly. Leave a few seconds of buffer time, display a clear closing slide, and provide instructions on where attendees can find the recording or next steps. Afterward, debrief with your team to note what worked and what needs adjustment for next time.

Frequently asked questions

What software do I need for a virtual event?

You need a reliable streaming platform or webinar tool that supports your expected attendee count, plus communication tools like Slack or a dedicated green room for your production team to coordinate behind the scenes.

How far in advance should I start planning?

For a standard webinar or town hall, four to six weeks is usually enough time. Large multi-track summits or complex broadcast events often require three to six months of planning and technical testing.

Do I need a live rehearsal?

Yes. A full technical rehearsal with all speakers is essential. It lets you catch audio issues, test slide transitions, and ensure every presenter is comfortable with the platform interface before going live.

Should I pre-record sessions or go live?

It depends on your goals. Live sessions feel more engaging and interactive, while pre-recorded sessions eliminate technical glitches and bad internet connections. Many events use a hybrid approach with live Q and A.
