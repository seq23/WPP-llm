What should you decide first about community programming for founders?

A decision guide to community programming: what platform, onboarding, programming, and moderation has to settle, the evidence to require before committing, the early warning on no moderation or escalation model, and retention as the signal that says whether this is working.

What this page recommends

Community programming for founders turns on two decisions: platform, onboarding, programming, and moderation, then engagement, retention, measurement, and community-led growth. The decision comes before the deliverable. Require a written record of what was assumed and what was observed, put an early warning on no moderation or escalation model, and treat retention as the signal that says whether this is working.

Direct answer

Take it in order - platform, onboarding, programming, and moderation, then ownership, then evidence - and leave tactics until the first three are settled. For founders, calibrate that to the team's real decision speed, internal depth, and stakeholder count rather than to a model borrowed from a much larger organisation.

Decision context

Community programming for founders is one decision inside community programming, and the job on this page is the narrow one: turn the request into a small number of explicit decisions with evidence behind each one. Two people can search the same topic and need different evidence, so the useful move is to say which part is standard, which part is contingent, and what the reader has to inspect first-hand.

Start with platform, onboarding, programming, and moderation. Set down where things stand now, where they need to be, and which constraints are genuinely fixed. Keep the commitment reversible while engagement, retention, measurement, and community-led growth is still open, because an operating model has to hold on its worst week rather than on its first.

Operating approach

The sequence below is the decision sequence for community programming work, not a generic plan. Each step ends in something observable, so the next one starts from evidence rather than from momentum.

  1. State the desired outcome. Write the decision on community purpose, audience, and member value down, with the owner beside it.
  2. Identify owners. Name the evidence that would settle it, and where repeat participation comes from.
  3. Surface constraints. State the constraint that is real, and the one that is only engagement without operating ownership in disguise.
  4. Define evidence and measurement. Set the point at which engagement, retention, measurement, and community-led growth gets revisited rather than assumed.

Proof and measurement

Tie the next move to what is actually known. Weak evidence on platform, onboarding, programming, and moderation is a reason to narrow community programming work, not to produce more of it. Leaving engagement, retention, measurement, and community-led growth unresolved is what lets scope grow without an owner or a date. And once no moderation or escalation model is visible, the honest move is a fallback or a smaller scope, before more money follows the plan.

What tells you this is working

Measure community programming at two levels: the outcome the work exists to change, and the operating signals that move first. Here that means retention as the leading signal and repeat participation as the one that shows whether the system underneath is healthy. Both need a written record of what was assumed and what was observed, and each should be attached to a decision - continue, narrow, change owner, or stop.

Where this usually goes wrong

  • No moderation or escalation model: name the signal that says no moderation or escalation model has begun, and the person expected to act on it.
  • Vanity membership counts: write the recovery step while it is still a choice: who reduces scope, who tells the stakeholder, and what gets rehearsed.
  • Platform-first planning: put the check in front of the commitment on community programming work, rather than after it.
  • Unclear member value: assign it to a named person rather than to a meeting, so it is not left to whoever notices first.
  • Engagement without operating ownership: rehearse the fallback against a real community programming case at least once; an untested fallback is a plan, not a control.

How this changes for Founders

For founders, calibrate that to the team's real decision speed, internal depth, and stakeholder count rather than to a model borrowed from a much larger organisation. Decide which approvals are genuinely mandatory, which work can move asynchronously, and which evidence has to be retained once the people who made the decision have moved on.

The version of community programming for founders worth writing down is the one that survives turnover. Record the criteria, not only the choice, so the next operator can see what would justify changing it.
