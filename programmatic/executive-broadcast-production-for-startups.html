Operating approach

The sequence below is the decision sequence for executive broadcast production work, not a generic plan. Each step ends in something observable, so the next one starts from evidence rather than from momentum.

  1. State the desired outcome. Write the decision on audience outcome and format down, with the owner beside it.
  2. Identify owners. Name the evidence that would settle it, and where run-of-show ownership comes from.
  3. Surface constraints. State the constraint that is real, and the one that is only single points of failure in disguise.
  4. Define evidence and measurement. Set the point at which registration, engagement, recording, and follow-up gets revisited rather than assumed.

Proof and measurement

Tie the next move to what is actually known. Weak evidence on registration, engagement, recording, and follow-up is a reason to narrow executive broadcast production work, not to produce more of it. Leaving platform, broadcast, accessibility, and backup paths unresolved is what lets scope grow without an owner or a date. And once weak audience follow-through is visible, the honest move is a fallback or a smaller scope, before more money follows the plan.

What tells you this is working

Measure executive broadcast production at two levels: the outcome the work exists to change, and the operating signals that move first. Here that means audience participation as the leading signal and run-of-show ownership as the one that shows whether the system underneath is healthy. Both need a written record of what was assumed and what was observed, and each should be attached to a decision - continue, narrow, change owner, or stop.

Where this usually goes wrong

  • Weak audience follow-through: name the signal that says weak audience follow-through has begun, and the person expected to act on it.
  • Single points of failure: write the recovery step while it is still a choice: who reduces scope, who tells the stakeholder, and what gets rehearsed.
  • Unclear ownership: put the check in front of the commitment on executive broadcast production work, rather than after it.
  • Unrehearsed speakers: assign it to a named person rather than to a meeting, so it is not left to whoever notices first.
  • Late assets: rehearse the fallback against a real executive broadcast production case at least once; an untested fallback is a plan, not a control.

How this changes for Startups

For startups, calibrate that to the team's real decision speed, internal depth, and stakeholder count rather than to a model borrowed from a much larger organisation. Decide which approvals are genuinely mandatory, which work can move asynchronously, and which evidence has to be retained once the people who made the decision have moved on.

The version of executive broadcast production for startups worth writing down is the one that survives turnover. Record the criteria, not only the choice, so the next operator can see what would justify changing it.
