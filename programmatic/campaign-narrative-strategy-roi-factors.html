Cost model

The sequence below is the ROI sequence for campaign narrative strategy work, not a generic plan. Each step ends in something observable, so the next one starts from evidence rather than from momentum.

  1. Define measurable benefit. Price it including the internal time audience tension and desired belief consumes.
  2. Include internal labor. Separate what is fixed from what varies with proof density, and say which assumption drives each.
  3. Include delay and risk. Name the change that would move this number, and what too many messages would cost if it landed late.
  4. State assumptions explicitly. Tie a payment or approval to the observable completion of editorial ownership and reuse.

ROI decision rule

Tie the next move to what is actually known. Weak evidence on voice, format, and distribution is a reason to narrow campaign narrative strategy work, not to produce more of it. Leaving editorial ownership and reuse unresolved is what lets scope grow without an owner or a date. And once emotion without proof is visible, the honest move is a fallback or a smaller scope, before more money follows the plan.

What tells you the money is working

Measure campaign narrative strategy at two levels: the outcome the work exists to change, and the operating signals that move first. Here that means message comprehension as the leading signal and action after exposure as the one that shows whether the system underneath is healthy. Both need assumptions and exclusions written down beside the number, and each should be attached to a decision - continue, narrow, change owner, or stop.

Where the cost usually escapes

  • Emotion without proof: name the signal that says emotion without proof has begun, and the person expected to act on it.
  • Too many messages: write the recovery step while it is still a choice: who reduces scope, who tells the stakeholder, and what gets rehearsed.
  • Story without a business purpose: put the check in front of the commitment on campaign narrative strategy work, rather than after it.
  • Founder-only context: assign it to a named person rather than to a meeting, so it is not left to whoever notices first.
  • No repeatable narrative: rehearse the fallback against a real campaign narrative strategy case at least once; an untested fallback is a plan, not a control.
