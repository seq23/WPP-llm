What should you decide first about platforms to host virtual events?

A decision guide to unclustered: what roles, run of show, rehearsal, and escalation has to settle, the evidence to require before committing, the early warning on late assets, and run-of-show ownership as the signal that says whether this is working.

What this page recommends

Platforms to host virtual events turns on two decisions: roles, run of show, rehearsal, and escalation, then registration, engagement, recording, and follow-up. The decision comes before the deliverable. Require a written record of what was assumed and what was observed, put an early warning on late assets, and treat run-of-show ownership as the signal that says whether this is working.

Direct answer

Take it in order - roles, run of show, rehearsal, and escalation, then ownership, then evidence - and leave tactics until the first three are settled. How far to take each step depends on how reversible the commitment is, and on what late assets would cost to fix late.

Decision context

Platforms to host virtual events is one decision inside unclustered, and the job on this page is the narrow one: turn the request into a small number of explicit decisions with evidence behind each one. Two people can search the same topic and need different evidence, so the useful move is to say which part is standard, which part is contingent, and what the reader has to inspect first-hand.

Start with roles, run of show, rehearsal, and escalation. Set down where things stand now, where they need to be, and which constraints are genuinely fixed. Keep the commitment reversible while registration, engagement, recording, and follow-up is still open, because an operating model has to hold on its worst week rather than on its first.

Operating approach

The sequence below is the decision sequence for unclustered work, not a generic plan. Each step ends in something observable, so the next one starts from evidence rather than from momentum.

  1. State the desired outcome. Write the decision on audience outcome and format down, with the owner beside it.
  2. Identify owners. Name the evidence that would settle it, and where run-of-show ownership comes from.
  3. Surface constraints. State the constraint that is real, and the one that is only single points of failure in disguise.
  4. Define evidence and measurement. Set the point at which registration, engagement, recording, and follow-up gets revisited rather than assumed.

Proof and measurement

Tie the next move to what is actually known. Weak evidence on roles, run of show, rehearsal, and escalation is a reason to narrow unclustered work, not to produce more of it. Leaving registration, engagement, recording, and follow-up unresolved is what lets scope grow without an owner or a date. And once late assets is visible, the honest move is a fallback or a smaller scope, before more money follows the plan.

What tells you this is working

Measure unclustered at two levels: the outcome the work exists to change, and the operating signals that move first. Here that means run-of-show ownership as the leading signal and audience participation as the one that shows whether the system underneath is healthy. Both need a written record of what was assumed and what was observed, and each should be attached to a decision - continue, narrow, change owner, or stop.

Where this usually goes wrong

  • Late assets: name the signal that says late assets has begun, and the person expected to act on it.
  • Unrehearsed speakers: write the recovery step while it is still a choice: who reduces scope, who tells the stakeholder, and what gets rehearsed.
  • Unclear ownership: put the check in front of the commitment on unclustered work, rather than after it.
  • Single points of failure: assign it to a named person rather than to a meeting, so it is not left to whoever notices first.
  • Weak audience follow-through: rehearse the fallback against a real unclustered case at least once; an untested fallback is a plan, not a control.
