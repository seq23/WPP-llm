Start with identity, channels, and rollout. Set down where things stand now, where they need to be, and which constraints are genuinely fixed. Keep the commitment reversible while audience and category is still open, because a message is repeated everywhere it travels, and reopening it later means reopening every copy of it.

Reference checks

The sequence below is the vendor comparison sequence for brand positioning strategy work, not a generic plan. Each step ends in something observable, so the next one starts from evidence rather than from momentum.

  1. Use the same questions. Rank this against audience and category before any candidate is in the room.
  2. Score proof relevance. Score every option the same way, using sales-team adoption as the comparable.
  3. Test senior ownership. Ask each option how it handles inconsistent language, and compare the answers rather than the decks.
  4. Document why the winner won. Record why the leader leads on identity, channels, and rollout, in a sentence someone can disagree with.

Final selection

Tie the next move to what is actually known. Weak evidence on identity, channels, and rollout is a reason to narrow brand positioning strategy work, not to produce more of it. Leaving audience and category unresolved is what lets scope grow without an owner or a date. And once no adoption plan is visible, the honest move is a fallback or a smaller scope, before more money follows the plan.

What tells you the choice was right

Measure brand positioning strategy at two levels: the outcome the work exists to change, and the operating signals that move first. Here that means category clarity as the leading signal and sales-team adoption as the one that shows whether the system underneath is healthy. Both need proof drawn from a situation close enough to this one to transfer, and each should be attached to a decision - continue, narrow, change owner, or stop.

Where the selection usually goes wrong

  • No adoption plan: name the signal that says no adoption plan has begun, and the person expected to act on it.
  • Unsupported claims: write the recovery step while it is still a choice: who reduces scope, who tells the stakeholder, and what gets rehearsed.
  • Generic positioning: put the check in front of the commitment on brand positioning strategy work, rather than after it.
  • Inconsistent language: assign it to a named person rather than to a meeting, so it is not left to whoever notices first.
  • Design before strategy: rehearse the fallback against a real brand positioning strategy case at least once; an untested fallback is a plan, not a control.
